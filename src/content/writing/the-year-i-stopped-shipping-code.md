title: The year I stopped shipping code
description: >-
  Moving from engineer to technical lead removed the feedback loop I had been running on
  for seven years. Nobody warned me about the withdrawal.
published: 2025-08-24
tags: ['career', 'leadership', 'engineering-management']

I became a technical lead in 2025. The job description said things about design guidance,
delivery, and ceremonies, all of which turned out to be accurate and none of which prepared
me for the actual adjustment.

The adjustment is this: for seven years I had a reliable daily signal that I had done
something. Tests went green. A thing that did not work now worked. That feedback arrives on
a timescale of hours and it is genuinely addictive.

Then the job changed and the signal went away.

## The output problem

A lead's contribution shows up in other people's work, delayed and diffused. You spend a
morning in a design discussion, and whether it was worth anything becomes apparent in three
weeks, filtered through six other decisions, and by then you cannot isolate your
contribution from everything else that happened.

For the first few months I compensated badly. I picked up implementation tasks I should have
delegated, because finishing them felt like proof. It was proof. It was also me being the
bottleneck on four things while doing a fifth thing that someone else could have done better
with more context.

The genuinely uncomfortable realisation was that **on my best days as a lead I write no code
at all, and I have to be okay with that.** Not because coding is beneath the role — I still
review, prototype, and get hands-on with production problems — but because the highest
leverage available to me is usually a conversation, not a commit.

## What replaced it

Three things, once I stopped fighting.

**Code review became the main instrument.** Not gatekeeping — teaching. A review that says
"this works, but here is why the other shape will hurt less in six months" is the highest
bandwidth teaching moment available, because it happens on real code the person cares about
right now. I started spending real time on reviews instead of treating them as an
interruption, and it is the single change that did the most for the team.

**Design discussion is where the expensive mistakes get prevented.** An hour spent
translating a vague business requirement into a concrete low-level design saves weeks. This
is the least visible work I do and by some distance the most valuable. It also never feels
productive while it is happening, which is why it is easy to skip.

**Saying no became a technical skill.** Every accepted request has a cost paid by someone
else's focus. Learning to trace that cost out loud — not "we can't," but "here is what that
displaces and here is when it could land" — turned out to be more about systems thinking
than about diplomacy.

## What I got wrong

**I under-communicated context.** I would make a decision with good reasons and pass down
the decision without the reasons. Then people implemented the letter of it and were confused
when I pushed back on the result. The reasoning is the useful part; the conclusion is just
its shadow.

**I confused availability with support.** Being reachable for every question is not
mentoring, it is a service desk. It also trains people not to develop judgement, because the
faster path is always to ask. Some of the best support I gave was being slightly slower to
answer.

**I kept the interesting work.** Not deliberately, but the gnarly problems are the fun ones,
and I reached for them out of habit. Those are exactly the problems that grow people. Handing
over the thing you most want to do yourself is the tax on the job.

## Would I go back?

No, but I understand people who do.

The trade is real. You exchange a fast, legible, personal feedback loop for a slow, diffuse,
collective one. If what you love about engineering is the loop itself, that is a bad trade
and there is no shame in saying so — the industry's habit of treating management as the only
promotion path has done real damage to people who were happier and more valuable building.

What I found on the other side is a different satisfaction, on a longer timescale: watching
someone on the team handle a production problem in a way they could not have six months ago,
and knowing that a review comment I left in March is somewhere in that.

It arrives much less often than a green test suite. It lasts longer.
