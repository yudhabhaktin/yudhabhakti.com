"Lead with empathy" is advice I received often and understood badly.

What I heard was: be considerate, protect people from pressure, do not make anyone
uncomfortable. I was good at that. I was also, for a stretch, not much use to the people I
was supposed to be developing, and it took someone telling me so for me to see it.

## The thing I got wrong

Empathy is an *accuracy* skill. It is the ability to build a correct model of someone else's
situation — what they are optimising for, what they are afraid of, what they know that you
do not, what pressure they are under from a direction you cannot see.

Niceness is a *comfort* behaviour. It is about how an interaction feels while it is
happening.

They overlap often enough that you can mistake one for the other for a long time. Where they
come apart is exactly where it matters: when the accurate thing to say is unwelcome.

I had an engineer whose work was consistently just below the standard the team needed.
Reviews took longer than they should. I knew it, and I handled it by leaving thorough,
gentle review comments and quietly doing more of the integration work myself. For months.
That felt kind. It was not kind — it was me choosing my own comfort in the conversation over
that person's ability to improve. They did not know where they stood, which is a genuinely
bad place to put someone, and the first honest conversation we had should have happened a
quarter earlier.

The accurate model of their situation was: *this person wants to be good at this and is not
being told what is missing.* Acting on that model meant an uncomfortable conversation. That
was the empathetic move. The gentle review comments were the opposite.

## What accurate empathy actually requires

**Knowing what someone is optimising for.** Not what you would optimise for in their seat.
An engineer six months from a visa renewal, a vendor team lead whose contract is measured on
milestones rather than quality, a stakeholder who has already promised a date upward — these
people are being rational inside constraints you may not have bothered to learn. Almost every
time I have privately concluded that someone was being unreasonable, I was missing a
constraint.

**Asking rather than inferring.** I am reasonably good at reading a room and this made me
overconfident. Inference from behaviour is a hypothesis, not a finding. "You seem
frustrated with this approach — am I reading that right?" costs nothing and has corrected me
more often than it has confirmed me.

**Separating the person's situation from your interpretation of it.** Someone missing
deadlines might be overloaded, blocked on something they are embarrassed to raise,
disengaged, or dealing with something outside work entirely. These need different responses,
and picking one without checking is not empathy, it is a guess wearing a costume.

## Where it is genuinely hard

**Across a vendor boundary.** I have worked with several external delivery teams. The
temptation is to treat them as a resource that either delivers or does not. But a vendor
engineer has a manager, a contract, and incentives that are frequently misaligned with what
you want, and none of that is their personal failing. Understanding those incentives changed
how I wrote requirements far more than any amount of escalation did.

**Across a hierarchy.** Empathy upward is discussed much less than empathy downward and is
at least as useful. An executive who keeps asking for a date is not being obtuse; they are
usually being asked for one by someone else and have no way to absorb the uncertainty you
are handing them. Once I started treating "when will it be done" as a request for
*something to commit to* rather than a demand for a fixed date, those conversations got
substantially easier — because I could offer a range, a confidence, and a checkpoint instead
of a flinch.

**When you are the constraint.** The hardest case. If someone is struggling because of a
decision you made, empathy requires modelling their situation accurately enough to see your
own contribution to it, which is precisely when motivated reasoning is strongest.

## The reframe I use now

The test I apply is: **do I understand this person's situation well enough to predict what
they will do next?** If yes, I have some empathy. If I am surprised by their behaviour
regularly, I do not, regardless of how considerate I have been.

That test has the useful property of being about accuracy rather than about how the
conversation felt. It also makes it obvious when I have substituted being liked for being
useful — because being liked does not improve prediction at all.

Hard feedback delivered by someone who clearly understands your situation is one of the more
valuable things you can receive at work. Soft feedback from someone who does not is noise.
I spent a year producing the second kind while believing I was doing the first.
